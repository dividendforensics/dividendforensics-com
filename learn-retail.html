Retail REITs: tenant sales, lease terms and redevelopment

Distinguish shopping centers and malls, then connect tenant sales and occupancy to the landlord's sustainable cash flow.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 October 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide

  4. Disclosures that reveal the cash bridge

Retail contains several businesses

Retail REITs can own grocery-anchored centers, malls, outlet centers and other shopping properties. A grocery visit, a luxury shopping trip and a discount-store visit create different tenant demand. Evaluate each property's customer catchment, competing destinations, accessibility and tenant mix. The number of stores alone does not reveal diversification: many tenants can depend on the same local household spending. Lease structures also differ, including expense reimbursements, percentage rent and provisions linked to anchor tenants or occupancy.

Follow the tenant's economics

Tenant sales can help explain whether rent is affordable, but disclosed sales often cover only a subset of tenants. Occupancy cost ratios need a clear denominator and consistent treatment of rent and other charges. A profitable store may support renewal increases; a weak location can close even when its parent company is healthy. Compare physical occupancy with leased occupancy, and identify signed leases that have not begun paying rent. Leasing success today can take months to translate into cash.

Competing views on physical retail

The constructive argument sees stores as part of an integrated shopping and fulfillment network, with strong locations drawing repeat visits. The cautious argument highlights retailer failures, consumer pressure and changing shopping habits. Redevelopment can make a center more useful but consumes cash and carries construction and permitting risk. Replacing an anchor with several smaller tenants may improve eventual rent while requiring long downtime. A strong long-term story should still be tested against near-term funding needs and realistic completion schedules.

Disclosures that reveal the cash bridge

Read new-lease and renewal spreads, tenant allowances, leasing commissions, anchor concentration, co-tenancy exposure and redevelopment commitments. Same-store NOI does not capture every cash cost and can exclude projects undergoing major work. Reconcile it with cash flow and recurring capital investment. When comparing AFFO, inspect whether recurring tenant costs are deducted and whether the treatment is consistent across years. Annual-report lease and risk disclosures help clarify provisions that a polished occupancy chart cannot show.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

A hypothetical store pays $100,000 annual base rent. A renewal raises that to $110,000, a 10% increase. If securing the five-year renewal requires a $50,000 landlord allowance, the simple annualized allowance is $10,000. The added rent and that allowance offset each other before financing, commissions and timing effects. This is not a discounted valuation; it illustrates why a positive rent spread can produce little incremental cash value. Expense reimbursements and sales-based rent would require separate modeling.

A common mistake

A shopping center with rising contractual rent can still consume cash through redevelopment and leasing costs. Sales, occupancy and rent spreads describe different parts of the picture.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Identify the retail format and catchment.
  • Check the sample behind tenant sales.
  • Compare leased and paying occupancy.
  • Read allowances, commissions and co-tenancy terms.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
