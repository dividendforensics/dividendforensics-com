BDC price / NAV discount calculator

Compare a user-entered share price with disclosed common NAV per share while preserving dates and assumptions.

By Jeong-Mo Goo · Published 18 October 2026 · Updated 18 October 2026

The question to answer.

At the entered price, what premium or discount is implied relative to the entered common NAV per share?

Educational scenario, not a forecast or investment recommendation. All prefilled amounts are synthetic. Editing them creates an unverified user scenario. Calculations stay in this browser tab; no inputs are stored or transmitted.

Your assumptions

Inputs are directly entered per-common-share values. No total-amount or weighted-average-share conversion is performed.

The analysis as-of date must be on or after the reporting period end. For balance-sheet stock measures, period fields identify the reporting context, not an averaging interval. Preserve actual snapshot and market-quote dates in each source note.

Keep every input on the period, basis and unit selected below. Source links are notes for your own audit, not a claim of verification.

Blank = not found. Enter 0 only for an explicit zero or a stated zero scenario. Manual input is not verification.

Define the inputs before comparing.

NAV per share represents the common net-asset base divided by the applicable common-share count under the issuer disclosure. The market price is a traded quotation at a different moment. The difference between the two is a valuation relationship, not a promised gain. A BDC invests in loans and other securities, and a disclosed fair-value estimate may differ from a later realization. Use the same share class and currency for both inputs.

The calculation.

Price/NAV = entered market price per common share / common NAV per share. Signed premium or discount = (price/NAV − 1) × 100. Price less NAV = entered price − NAV. A negative signed percentage is a discount; a positive value is a premium. Positive NAV is required for the first two ratios.

Formula version bdc-1.0.1. Decimal arithmetic uses 40 significant digits without intermediate display rounding. Results display four decimal places, half up; very small nonzero values use scientific notation. Blank or unavailable inputs stay missing; zero denominators produce “Not defined” with a reason.

Synthetic example — not company results

Reproduce the arithmetic.

Synthetic per-share illustration in USD: an entered price of 18 and common NAV per share of 20 produce price/NAV of 0.9000× and a signed discount of −10.0000%. Price less NAV is −2.0000. If price is 22 with NAV unchanged at 20, the signed result is a +10.0000% premium. This is not the same as the return needed to rise from 18 to 20, which would use 18 as its denominator.

Where to find the inputs.

Find the common NAV per share in the financial statements, statement of assets and liabilities or financial highlights. Record the reporting date, share class, issuer calculation and any later distribution or issuance affecting comparability. Record the market-price date and source in the price input note. There is no automatic quote service: the reader supplies both values. The common as-of context is an analysis date and must be on or after the reporting period end. NAV is a stock measure at its disclosed balance-sheet snapshot date, not income over the entered period. The market quote can have a different observation date: retain both the NAV snapshot date and the actual quote date in their respective source locators. The analysis date does not make these two observations contemporaneous and does not relabel either as a period-end quote.

Record each source URL, accession if applicable, table or note location, original period and scale. Preserve the issuer definition. The source record below each result distinguishes manual entry from review: no reviewer or review date is invented.

Interpret the result with its denominator.

A discount can reflect expected losses, expenses, financing conditions, management incentives, liquidity or the market assessment of the valuation process. It is not an automatic bargain. A premium is not proof of overvaluation. Compare valuation dates and the underlying portfolio rather than treating the ratio as a trading signal. Investor.gov explains that publicly traded BDC prices can be above or below NAV; the calculator only quantifies this relationship for your inputs.

What this worksheet leaves out.

No appraisal, target price or expected return is produced. NAV can become stale and the price can move after entry. The tool does not adjust for subsequent distributions, rights issues, stock splits, FX or changes in portfolio value. Zero or negative NAV makes a conventional premium/discount ratio unhelpful and is shown as undefined while the arithmetic price-minus-NAV difference remains visible.

No automatic currency, share-count, quarter/YTD or adjusted-to-reported conversion occurs. The common context is a user assertion, not evidence that pasted figures match. Check each original disclosure yourself; a manual input is not a verified company result.

Questions worth asking.

Is a 20% discount a guaranteed 20% gain?

No. NAV and price can both change, and the discount need not close.

Does this tool fetch a current share price?

No. Every figure is entered by the reader; the initial example is synthetic.
