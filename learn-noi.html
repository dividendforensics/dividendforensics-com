NOI: separate the property from its financing

Build a property operating view and understand why positive NOI does not guarantee positive shareholder cash flow.

The property-level question

Net operating income asks what a property earns after its operating expenses, before the financing and corporate layers. A simple model subtracts property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, and other property operating costs from property revenue. Issuer definitions can vary, including treatment of straight-line rent and particular reimbursements. NOI is useful because two identical buildings should not appear operationally different simply because one owner borrowed more. It does not represent the income available to common shareholders after all obligations.

Trace revenues and expenses together

A gross lease may show substantial tenant reimbursements and substantial reimbursable expenses. A net lease can leave many obligations directly with the tenant. Comparing revenue margins without understanding those structures can mislead. Read who actually bears taxes, insurance, repairs, and capital replacements. Also separate billed rent from collected rent, contractual rent from concessions, and occupied space from paying space. A favorable revenue trend can conceal a larger insurance increase or a temporary reimbursement settlement that will not recur.

Growth and quality are different questions

An expansion-minded reader may celebrate total NOI growth because new acquisitions add income. An operating reader asks whether the existing portfolio improved without acquisitions. Both are legitimate, but answer different questions. Same-store analysis helps isolate an eligible comparable pool, while total NOI explains the current enterprise. Neither captures all capital required to maintain the income stream. A landlord may sustain NOI by spending heavily on tenant improvements outside property operating expense. Review the cost of maintaining occupancy as well as the occupancy itself.

How to reconstruct the story

Find the property operating table and its reconciliation in the supplemental report or filing. List included revenue and expense categories and note cash versus accounting presentation. Build a bridge from prior-period NOI using rent, occupancy, expenses, acquisitions, dispositions, and currency. Then connect NOI to corporate expenses, interest, and recurring investment. This second bridge makes clear why healthy buildings can belong to a financially stressed owner. Use a full year and comparable seasons when utility expenses or occupancy vary sharply across quarters.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical arithmetic: NOI is only the first layer

Imagine annual rent and reimbursements of $12 million and property operating expenses of $4 million. NOI is $8 million. Interest of $3 million, allocated corporate costs of $1 million, and recurring capital needs of $2 million leave $2 million before other cash movements and obligations. If property expenses rise 10% while revenue stays flat, NOI falls to $7.6 million: a 5% decline. The effect on the simplified residual is a 20% decline, from $2 million to $1.6 million.

A common mistake

Treating NOI as free cash flow skips financing, corporate overhead, capital expenditure, and other claims.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Read the NOI definition and reconciliation.
  • Identify lease expense responsibilities.
  • Separate total from same-store growth.
  • Bridge property income to owner cash needs.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
