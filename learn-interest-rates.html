REITs and interest rates: more than one transmission channel

Separate financing costs, property valuation, tenant demand and rent growth when thinking about interest-rate changes.

Debt costs move on a schedule

A change in market interest rates does not instantly reprice every dollar of a REIT's debt. Fixed-rate borrowings remain at their contractual rates until refinancing or another relevant event. Floating-rate debt may respond sooner, subject to the benchmark, spread, floors and hedging. Read the debt maturity schedule and derivative notes together. A low average coupon can describe the existing book while concealing a much higher rate on the next borrowing.

Property values depend on required returns

Investors may demand higher yields from property when alternative yields rise, but the relationship is not mechanical. Property cap rates also reflect growth expectations, risk, transaction evidence and asset quality. In a simplified constant-NOI calculation, a higher cap rate lowers value. Real property income can change at the same time. Applying a single cap rate to a diverse portfolio can therefore obscure more than it explains.

The operating backdrop can pull the other way

Higher rates may accompany stronger nominal growth and opportunities to raise rents. Short leases can reset faster but also expose landlords to weaker demand more quickly. Long leases can stabilize contractual revenue while fixed escalators lag inflation. If rate cuts arrive during a recession, cheaper financing may be offset by tenant stress and vacancies. The direction of rates alone is not an investment thesis.

Use a bridge from rate change to per-share outcome

Start with the amount of debt actually repricing, multiply by the change in effective rate, and translate the incremental interest into an approximate per-share effect. Then examine rental growth, asset sales, hedges and equity issuance separately. This avoids assuming that a favorable funding change benefits each share equally. New shares can spread the benefit more widely, and accounting classifications may differ from cash payments. Check management's guidance assumptions and the maturity note before extending a simple sensitivity into a forecast.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical: a refinancing step-up

If $200 million of debt moves from 3% to 5%, annual interest rises by $4 million before fees, hedges or changes in principal. With 100 million common shares, that is $0.04 per share of additional annual interest. It is an isolated sensitivity; operations, taxes and the issuer's adjusted measures may produce a different overall result.

A common mistake

REITs do not uniformly rise when rates fall or fall when rates rise. Debt structure, growth expectations and the reason rates changed matter.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Separate fixed, floating and hedged debt.
  • Identify maturities rather than applying the new rate to all debt.
  • Stress cap rates and NOI independently.
  • Translate financing changes into per-share terms.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
