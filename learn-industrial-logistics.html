Industrial REITs: warehouses, rent resets and supply

Learn how logistics properties earn rent and why lease spreads, building specifications and development exposure need separate analysis.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide

Buildings within a distribution network

Industrial REITs own facilities used for manufacturing, storage and distribution. Warehouses serving ports, regional networks and local delivery routes can have different economics. Location matters alongside clear height, loading docks, yard space, power availability and road access. A building suited to one operation may not attract the same rent from another tenant. E-commerce is one demand source, but the sector also depends on inventory decisions, trade flows and the health of business customers.

Understand the rent-reset opportunity

A lease signed years earlier may be below today's market rent. Management may call that gap embedded growth or mark-to-market opportunity. It becomes cash only when the lease resets, a customer agrees to pay and occupancy is maintained. Cash leasing spreads compare contractual cash rents; accounting-based spreads can reflect straight-line recognition. Neither measure alone tells you how many leases will expire, how much downtime is expected or what leasing incentives and building improvements are needed.

Competing views on demand and construction

The favorable case emphasizes scarce infill locations and expensive or impractical alternatives for tenants. The skeptical case emphasizes new competing facilities, slower goods demand and customers releasing excess space. Both can be true in different markets. Construction starts, deliveries and vacancy should be assessed locally and over several quarters. A recently completed facility can command strong rent yet take time to stabilize. Development creates potential value but also adds cost overruns, leasing uncertainty and a need for funding before rent arrives.

Read beyond portfolio occupancy

Inspect lease-expiration schedules, retention, tenant concentration, signed-but-not-commenced leases and development commitments. Separate properties owned outright from joint ventures; fee income and proportionate property results do not necessarily represent the same cash flows. Check whether same-store NOI excludes developments and recent acquisitions, and whether leasing spreads cover comparable spaces. AFFO adjustments require particular attention to recurring capital needs and tenant improvements. Sustainable per-share progress depends on the cost of financing acquisitions and developments as well as headline property growth.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

Suppose 10% of a hypothetical portfolio's $20 million annual rent rolls this year and those leases reset 20% higher. With immediate full occupancy, the annualized increase is $20 million × 10% × 20% = $400,000, or 2% of portfolio rent. If resets happen halfway through the year, the first-year contribution is about $200,000 before downtime, incentives and costs. A 20% leasing spread is therefore not 20% portfolio revenue growth.

A common mistake

Do not apply a leasing spread to the entire rent base or assume every modern warehouse benefits equally from the same logistics trend.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Check cash versus accounting spreads.
  • Locate upcoming expirations by market.
  • Include tenant improvements and downtime.
  • Review development funding and joint ventures.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
