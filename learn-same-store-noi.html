Same-store NOI: test growth inside the existing portfolio

Understand comparable-property pools, cash versus accounting growth, and the exclusions that can change the story.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. What the comparable pool solves
  2. Worked example
  3. Look underneath the growth percentage
  4. Comparable does not mean complete
  5. A reproducible reading workflow
  6. Sources & next steps
01

What the comparable pool solves

Same-store NOI compares a defined set of properties across periods to reduce distortion from acquisitions and dispositions. It helps answer whether an existing operating portfolio improved. The phrase does not describe one universal pool: issuers set eligibility rules for ownership duration, stabilization, redevelopment, and other conditions. Read the population definition before comparing growth rates. A same-store result may cover only part of the economic business and should sit beside total property income, not replace it.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical arithmetic: acquisitions mask a decline

An eligible same-store pool produces $50 million NOI last year and $49 million this year, a 2% decline. Newly acquired properties add $10 million, taking total NOI from $50 million to $59 million, an 18% increase in this simplified case. Both statements are true: the enterprise is larger and the comparable pool weakened. If management excludes a redevelopment property, analyze its income loss and project spending separately rather than quietly adding it to one side of the comparison.

02

Look underneath the growth percentage

Break the movement into rental rates, occupancy, concessions, bad debt, reimbursements, and operating expenses. Cash measures can differ from accounting measures because of straight-line rent and other noncash items. Currency translation and ownership changes can also affect multinational or joint-venture portfolios. A percentage becomes more informative when the company explains these drivers. Strong rent growth can coexist with weaker occupancy, while lower property taxes can temporarily improve NOI despite flat demand. Distinguish repeatable operating improvements from unusually easy comparisons.

03

Comparable does not mean complete

An operational view values a stable pool because it separates management of existing buildings from acquisition spending. A skeptical view asks whether exclusions remove the hardest properties. Redevelopment exclusions can be reasonable when a building changes substantially, but they also mean the metric omits part of the owner's risk and capital needs. Examine excluded assets separately rather than declaring the metric invalid. Review the percentage of total NOI represented by the pool and changes in membership across years. A shrinking pool deserves an explanation.

04

A reproducible reading workflow

Download the supplemental report, locate the population footnote, and record both current and comparison-period NOI. Check whether results are consolidated, proportionate, or reported at full venture ownership. Recalculate the growth rate and compare cash with accounting versions. Then build a short driver note and a list of exclusions. For cross-company comparisons, preserve differences in sector and definitions. Finally, connect the operating result with recurring capital expenditure: stronger same-store NOI does not disclose what was spent outside operating expense to obtain it.

A common mistake

Comparing same-store growth without checking pool eligibility, ownership share, and cash adjustments can produce a false ranking.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Read the comparable-pool footnote.
  • Recompute the stated percentage.
  • Compare cash and accounting measures.
  • Examine excluded properties and capital spending.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
