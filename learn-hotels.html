Hotel REITs: RevPAR, operating leverage and renovations

Connect hotel room pricing and occupancy with operating profit, brand obligations and the cash required to keep properties competitive.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. Rooms reprice quickly
  2. The three room metrics
  3. Competing views on rapid repricing
  4. Capital spending and issuer checks
  5. Worked example
  6. Sources & next steps
01

Rooms reprice quickly

Hotel REITs own properties serving leisure, business and group travelers. Their economic exposure differs from a landlord collecting fixed multiyear rent because room revenue changes as guests book and stay. Ownership, brand and management arrangements can separate the real-estate owner from day-to-day hotel operations; inspect the actual structure. Location, service level, meeting space and guest mix influence performance. A resort, airport hotel and urban convention property can react differently to the same economic conditions.

02

The three room metrics

Occupancy measures rooms sold relative to available rooms. Average daily rate, or ADR, is room revenue divided by rooms sold. Revenue per available room, or RevPAR, combines occupancy and ADR. It helps describe room performance but excludes parts of the business such as food, beverages and other services, and says nothing directly about costs. Comparisons also depend on consistent room availability and treatment of renovations or closures. Revenue growth driven by price can have different cost implications from growth driven by occupied rooms.

03

Competing views on rapid repricing

The optimistic argument is that hotels can respond quickly to strong demand and higher prices. The cautious argument is that weak demand also reaches revenue quickly, while many operating costs cannot be removed at the same speed. Labor, insurance, utilities, distribution commissions and management fees affect margins. Strong leisure demand may not offset weak meetings at a group-oriented property. Examine demand segments and booking timing rather than applying one travel narrative to every hotel or treating a strong holiday period as a full-year trend.

04

Capital spending and issuer checks

Review comparable-property definitions, occupancy, ADR, RevPAR, hotel-level profit and corporate expense separately. Reconcile adjusted measures with reported results, and distinguish recurring refurbishment from expansion projects. Brand-required renovations can consume cash and temporarily remove rooms from service. Debt maturities and liquidity matter because revenue is variable while financing obligations continue. AFFO is not standardized; a measure that adds back depreciation without realistic recurring capital allowances can overstate the cash available to owners. Check management agreements and renovation commitments in the annual report.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

A hypothetical 200-room hotel has 75% occupancy and $160 ADR for a 365-day year. RevPAR is 75% × $160 = $120, and annual room revenue is 200 × 365 × $120 = $8.76 million. If occupancy falls to 70% and ADR rises to $165, RevPAR falls to $115.50 and room revenue to $8.4315 million. That is a 3.75% revenue decline despite a higher room rate, before considering other revenue, operating costs or renovations.

A common mistake

RevPAR is a revenue indicator, not profit or dividend coverage. Hotels with equal RevPAR can have materially different labor costs, service obligations and capital needs.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Compare occupancy, ADR and RevPAR together.
  • Separate leisure, business and group exposure.
  • Read renovation and brand obligations.
  • Bridge hotel profit to corporate cash flow.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
