Dilution: follow your share of the business

Distinguish a rising share count from harmful economic dilution and evaluate acquisition growth per share.

DFB Education · Advanced learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. More shares do not answer the whole question
  2. Separate earnings and asset-value effects
  3. Growth and resilience can conflict
  4. Follow the filing trail
  5. Worked example
  6. Sources & next steps

More shares do not answer the whole question

Issuing common stock increases the number of ownership claims. Whether it improves or weakens the economics of each existing share depends on the price received, issuance costs, and use of proceeds. REITs may raise equity to buy properties, fund development, or reduce debt. Total assets and total FFO can rise while FFO per share falls. Start with the per-share result, then examine risk and future investment requirements. A larger company is not automatically a better investment for each owner.

Separate earnings and asset-value effects

An issuance can be accretive to a short-term earnings measure yet unfavorable under a particular NAV estimate, or the reverse. Earnings accretion depends on the incremental return after financing and operating costs. NAV impact depends on the assumed asset values and issue price. Because NAV itself is uncertain, present this analysis as a scenario. Also consider whether the transaction reduces leverage, broadens tenants, or creates execution risk. One numerical measure cannot summarize every change in the shareholder's position.

Growth and resilience can conflict

A growth-focused management team may emphasize acquisitions made possible by fresh capital. An existing holder may question why stock was issued at a depressed price. Sometimes raising equity protects the business against expensive refinancing, even when near-term per-share earnings decline. Sometimes appealing acquisition headlines conceal weak returns. Evaluate the alternative funding choices and their risks. Ask whether the benefit is visible in durable per-share income, improved financing flexibility, or a credible project schedule rather than assuming every share issuance is either harmful or beneficial.

Follow the filing trail

Read the equity note, cash-flow financing section, stock-compensation disclosure, and earnings reconciliation. Distinguish shares issued during the period, end-period shares, and weighted-average diluted shares used for reported earnings. Include relevant convertible instruments and operating-partnership units consistently. For a transaction, check the pro forma period and whether acquired earnings are annualized while issuance costs are excluded. Compare management's accretion expectation with later reported results, allowing for timing and unexpected operating changes. Keep your original assumptions so you can explain the difference.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical arithmetic: total growth, per-share decline

A REIT earns $100 million of annual FFO on 25 million shares, or $4 per share. It issues 5 million shares and adds $15 million of annual FFO after the modeled costs. Total FFO increases 15% to $115 million, but the share count rises 20% to 30 million. FFO per share becomes about $3.83, a 4.2% decline. Adding $25 million instead would produce about $4.17 per share. These simplified full-year cases ignore transaction timing and other adjustments.

A common mistake

Judging a transaction by total FFO growth ignores how many additional shares claim that income.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Compare total and per-share results.
  • Include fees and recurring costs.
  • Match income timing with weighted shares.
  • Consider leverage and realistic funding alternatives.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
