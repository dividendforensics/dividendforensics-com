Dividend yield versus total return: count both sides

Calculate income and price change together, and understand why a rising dividend yield can be a warning rather than a reward.

01

Yield describes a ratio at a price

Dividend yield divides an annual dividend amount by a share price. The numerator might be the most recent payment annualized, trailing twelve-month payments, or an analyst forecast. Those conventions can produce different yields for the same security. A special distribution can distort the trailing figure. Always identify the basis. Yield tells you how an assumed distribution compares with price; it does not tell you whether that distribution will continue.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical: 6% income, negative return

Buy one share for $100, receive $6 in distributions and finish the year with a share worth $90. Simple pretax total return is ($90 − $100 + $6) ÷ $100 = −4%. The initial 6% yield was an income ratio, not a promise of a positive return. Reinvestment, taxes and fees would change the realized result.

02

Total return includes the changing claim

For one share held over a period, a simple total return includes the ending price minus the starting price plus cash distributions, divided by the starting price. It ignores reinvestment timing, transaction costs and taxes unless explicitly adjusted. A high cash yield can coexist with a negative total return when the share price falls. Conversely, a lower-yielding business may generate stronger total return if per-share cash generation and market value rise.

03

Yield on cost answers a narrower question

Yield on cost compares today's annual dividend with your historical purchase price. It can help describe income growth on an original investment, but it is not the yield available to a new investor or the opportunity cost of keeping the position. Decisions made today involve today's market value. A long-held position with a high yield on cost can still face deteriorating coverage, expensive refinancing or concentration risk.

04

Compare scenarios rather than chasing the largest number

An income-oriented investor may prefer regular cash distributions, while another values reinvestment inside the business. Neither preference makes a headline yield a safety score. Model an unchanged dividend, a reduction and a price change separately. Reinvesting dividends buys more shares but does not erase the risk of a falling asset value. If you compare a REIT with a bond, remember that the common equity dividend and redemption value are not contractual bond payments.

A common mistake

A falling share price mechanically increases the indicated yield if the assumed dividend stays unchanged. It may be signaling risk that the current dividend does not yet reflect.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Specify trailing, indicated or forecast dividends.
  • Separate recurring distributions from special payments.
  • Show price change alongside cash income.
  • Use consistent currencies and account for fees and tax assumptions.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
