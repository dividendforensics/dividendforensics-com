Ex-dividend date vs record date vs payment date

Understand US dividend dates under T+1, ordinary cash-dividend entitlement, special-distribution exceptions and why dividend capture is not free income.

DFB Education · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Sources and editorial approach

Four dates answer four different questions

The declaration date tells you when a dividend was announced. The ex-dividend date determines whether a normal purchase carries entitlement to that distribution. The record date identifies the holders on the company's records, and the payment date is when the distribution is payable. A payment calendar alone does not tell you whether a new purchase qualifies.

This guide concerns US securities and ordinary settlement. Always use the announced ex-date for the specific security and distribution. Special distributions, stock distributions, foreign securities and late announcements can require different treatment.

Dividend dates at a glance

Why older “one day before the record date” explanations can be wrong

Under current US T+1 settlement, the ordinary ex-date is generally the record date when that date is a business day. FINRA Rule 11140 specifies the normal rule for distributions below 25% of the security's value when definitive information arrives sufficiently early. If the record date is a designated non-delivery day, the rule uses the preceding business day. The exchange or relevant authority's designated ex-date controls.

Do not subtract a fixed number of calendar days from a payment date. Weekends, holidays and distribution-specific terms matter. The change to T+1 did not turn the dividend into additional economic value created by buying on a particular day. It changed the settlement timing relevant to ordinary entitlement. Keep the actual announcement or exchange notice with any calendar entry.

A hypothetical ordinary cash distribution

Assume a US-listed share announces a 0.50 USD cash dividend, below the 25% threshold. Its announced record date and ex-date are both Tuesday, October 20, 2026, and its payment date is Friday, October 30, 2026. Assume normal settlement and no applicable holiday or special arrangement.

  1. A normal purchase on Monday, October 19, is before the announced ex-date and carries that distribution.
  2. A normal purchase on Tuesday, October 20, is on the ex-date and does not carry that distribution, even though Tuesday is also the record date.
  3. The payment date is later. Buying merely before October 30 is not enough to establish entitlement.

This invented schedule is a reading exercise, not a live company calendar or an instruction to trade. Your broker can clarify account-specific processing and settlement questions.

Special dividends and due bills: read the exception first

FINRA's normal rule for distributions of 25% or more of the security's value places the ex-date on the first business day after the payable date. That means the ordinary “keep the payment because you held on record date” shortcut can fail. A due-bill arrangement may transfer entitlement with a trade. A distribution described casually as “special” is not by that label alone necessarily in the 25%-or-more category.

Stock distributions, ADRs, foreign securities and late information can have additional rules. Do not build a personal calendar by applying the ordinary cash-dividend example to every event. Locate the designated ex-date and the applicable terms, and ask the broker about due bills before relying on a balance appearing in an account.

Why dividend capture is not free income

In a deliberately simplified example, 100 shares at 25 USD have a market value of 2,500 USD. A 0.50 USD distribution produces 50 USD of cash. If the shares subsequently trade at 24.50 USD, their value is 2,450 USD; shares plus distribution are still 2,500 USD before fees and taxes. This is an illustration of value transfer, not a forecast of an exact market-price adjustment.

Actual prices also reflect news, market moves and trading conditions. A distribution can coincide with either a larger loss or a gain. Transaction costs, taxes and currency movements can further change the outcome. Compare price change and distributions together with the total return calculator, and read dividend yield versus total return before interpreting a calendar as a return strategy.

Dates, forecasts and dividend sustainability are different tasks

An ex-date tells you about one declared event. It does not establish that next quarter's dividend will be the same. An aggregator may estimate a future date from a previous schedule or annualize a payment that included a supplemental amount. Label a date as announced or estimated and keep regular and additional distributions separate. For a BDC, see regular and supplemental distributions; for a REIT, use the AFFO payout worksheet to investigate a matching-period scenario.

The dividend income calculator reports a monthly equivalent, not a payment calendar. A quarterly payer can have a monthly average of 100 USD and pay nothing in several months. Planning liquidity requires actual cash dates as well as a sustainable-income assumption. Record the amount, currency, security class, declaration source, ex-date, record date and payable date separately in your research note.
