Stock basics · Beginner guide

Tickers, exchanges, indexes, and ETFs

DFB Education · Published 1 November 2026

How to read a market label

AAPL, NYSE, Nasdaq, and S&P 500 do not name the same kind of thing. AAPL is a ticker, NYSE is an exchange, and the S&P 500 is an index. Nasdaq can refer to an exchange or an index, depending on context. Learning the difference makes a quote page much easier to read.

Start with a simple question: is this the name of a security, the market where it is listed, a benchmark, or a fund?

A ticker identifies the security

A ticker symbol is the short identifier used to look up a security. AAPL identifies Apple’s common stock. Apple confirms that its shares trade on the Nasdaq Stock Market under that symbol. Apple investor FAQ.

If a quote page shows NASDAQ: AAPL, read it as “Apple stock, identified by AAPL, with Nasdaq as its listing exchange.” The exact punctuation varies by platform. This is a way to identify the listing, not a claim about the company’s size, dividend policy, or investment quality. Nasdaq’s Apple quote page.

A ticker is not always an individual company stock. Funds have tickers too. Read the full security name before assuming what you would own.

NYSE and Nasdaq are exchanges

NYSE means the New York Stock Exchange. The Nasdaq Stock Market is another exchange. Exchanges provide organized markets for securities trading and have listing requirements. They are not themselves categories such as “dividend stock” or “growth stock.” NYSE’s market overview.

An exchange label tells you about the listing. An index label tells you about a defined group being measured. A company can have both a listing exchange and membership in an index; those descriptions do not compete with each other.

Nasdaq can also mean an index

This is where the wording gets confusing. The Nasdaq exchange, the Nasdaq Composite, and the Nasdaq-100 are related names, but they are not interchangeable.

The Nasdaq Composite is a broad index of eligible Nasdaq-listed securities. It is not literally every product traded there: its methodology excludes types such as ETFs and preferred stocks. Nasdaq Composite methodology.

The Nasdaq-100 is a different index focused on large non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq. It uses its own selection rules. It is not the same basket as the Composite, and neither index is simply another name for the exchange. Nasdaq-100 overview.

When a headline says “Nasdaq rose,” check which index the article means. When a fund says it tracks a Nasdaq index, check the full benchmark name.

The S&P 500 is a benchmark rather than a fund

The S&P 500 is an index of leading large U.S. companies. It measures a defined basket of stocks; it is not an exchange or an individual company. S&P Dow Jones Indices’ overview.

An ETF, short for exchange-traded fund, is an investment product that pools money into a portfolio. An ETF can seek to track the S&P 500, but the ETF and the index remain different things. The index is the benchmark; the ETF is the product an investor can buy. Not every ETF follows an index. Investor.gov’s ETF introduction.

A few numbers you will see beside the name

Share price is a price for one share. Check whether the quote is current, delayed, or a previous close. A lower share price alone does not mean a company is cheaper relative to its earnings or assets.

Market cap estimates the market value of the company’s equity: share price multiplied by shares outstanding. It is not the same as the price of one share. Investor.gov’s market-cap definition.

EPS, or earnings per share, expresses company profit on a per-share basis. It is not the cash dividend paid to you. Investor.gov’s EPS definition.

P/E, or price-to-earnings ratio, compares share price with earnings per share. Check which earnings period the page uses before comparing two ratios. A low P/E alone is not proof of a bargain. Investor.gov’s P/E definition.

Dividend yield compares annual dividends per share with the share price. Check whether it uses past payments or an estimate based on the current payout. Yield can rise because the share price fell, and dividends can change: a displayed yield is not a guaranteed return. Fidelity’s dividend-yield explanation.

Read the label before the number

Identify the security, its listing, and any benchmark first. Then examine the figures and their definitions. Knowing what a label means helps you ask better questions; it does not replace researching the investment.
