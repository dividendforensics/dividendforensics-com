Debt maturities: read the calendar, not just the average

Map refinancing needs against cash, committed liquidity, lease risk, and future borrowing costs.

01

Why timing can dominate the debt ratio

Debt maturity analysis asks when principal must be repaid or refinanced. Two REITs with the same debt balance and current interest expense can face very different risks if one has a large repayment next year and the other has staggered obligations. A weighted-average maturity compresses that calendar into a single figure and can hide a near-term concentration. Read the annual buckets and material individual facilities. Include mortgage debt, unsecured notes, revolving credit, and the appropriate share of joint-venture obligations.

A common mistake

An attractive average maturity can conceal one large near-term repayment and an expiring credit facility.

What to check in the disclosures

  • List annual principal repayments.
  • Separate restricted and unrestricted liquidity.
  • Check extension conditions and covenant headroom.
  • Model higher rates and lower asset-sale proceeds.
02

Map the available funding sources

Compare upcoming principal with unrestricted cash, committed undrawn credit, retained operating funds, and realistically available asset-sale proceeds. A credit line can bridge a repayment but may itself mature soon, require covenant compliance, or carry floating rates. An asset sale is an assumption until completed and may reduce future income. Do not treat restricted cash as freely available or count the same facility twice. Read extension options carefully: the borrower may have to satisfy conditions, pay a fee, or maintain specified financial ratios.

03

Refinancing success can still reduce earnings

The optimistic case is that a sound borrower can refinance well before maturity and retain access to multiple capital markets. The cautious case recognizes that access and price are separate questions. Replacing cheap debt with more expensive debt can lower per-share earnings even when principal is fully refinanced. Selling assets or issuing equity may protect liquidity while changing future growth. Evaluate the cost of each route. A debt maturity is not automatically a crisis, but treating every maturity as costless is equally unhelpful.

04

A filing-based review

Begin with the debt footnote, then read liquidity, covenant disclosures, hedging notes, and events after the reporting date. Place maturities and hedge expirations on one timeline. Identify secured assets that cannot easily support another borrowing and distinguish fixed from floating exposure after hedges. Compare the financing calendar with major lease expirations and development payments. Use issuer investor materials as an index, then verify details in filed documents. Date the analysis because a bond issuance completed after year-end can materially change the picture.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical arithmetic: refinancing at a higher rate

Suppose $200 million of debt carrying 3% interest matures next year. Refinancing the full amount at an assumed 6% increases annual interest from $6 million to $12 million, a $6 million difference before fees or hedging. Across 30 million shares, that is $0.20 per share before other effects. If the company repays $50 million first, the new $150 million borrowing costs $9 million annually. The repayment uses liquidity and should not be modeled as free money.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
