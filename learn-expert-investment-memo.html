Expert workshop: write a falsifiable REIT research memo

Separate facts from assumptions, test opposing explanations, and turn a valuation spreadsheet into an auditable research document.

Prerequisites and the research question

Complete the AFFO-normalization, joint-venture NAV, and refinancing workshops before attempting this synthesis. A useful memo begins with a question narrow enough to disprove: can the modeled recurring earnings cover the ordinary dividend after known refinancing and realistic maintenance? It does not begin with a preferred share-price conclusion. Our fictional REIT, Harbor Example, is an educational case with no connection to a listed company. All amounts are millions of dollars except per-share figures. The memo will distinguish assumed case facts, analyst choices, arithmetic results, and unresolved evidence. It makes no buy or sell recommendation.

Create an evidence ledger before a thesis

Suppose the case packet supplies FFO of 130, noncash rent of 5, a deducted maintenance reserve of 8, a compensation add-back of 3, actual recurring maintenance and leasing spending of 16, and dividends of 90. Record each as a hypothetical input. In real work, attach the filing date, section or page, ownership basis, and period to every material number. A management statement that spending is temporary is a claim; your belief that it will normalize is an inference. Do not convert either into a historical fact. Missing evidence should remain visibly missing.

Construct two explanations that fit the headlines

Reported AFFO in the case is 130 − 5 − 8 + 3 = 120, suggesting 75% dividend payout. The optimistic explanation says the reserve represents normal needs and current heavy spending will fade. The skeptical explanation says the portfolio requires the actual 16 every year and stock compensation remains an owner cost. Under that consistent owner-earnings policy, normalized earnings are 130 − 5 − 16 = 109, and payout is 82.57%. The two narratives share the same underlying inputs but disagree about recurring expenditure. Identify the maintenance history and lease commitments that could resolve that disagreement.

Combine financing and operating stresses explicitly

Assume 300 of debt, already included in earnings at 3%, resets to 6% for the entire next year. The incremental cost is 9, reducing normalized earnings from 109 to 100 if everything else is flat. Dividends of 90 then imply 90% payout. A downside case loses another 12 of NOI and requires 4 more recurring expenditure, producing 84 and payout of 107.14%. An upside case adds 8 of NOI with no extra recurring investment, producing 108 and payout of 83.33%. These are conditional calculations, not assigned probabilities. Do not average them into expected earnings without a defensible probability model.

Put valuation and liquidity beside earnings

Assume base property NOI of 80, a 6.5% cap rate, cash of 25, total debt of 500 including the refinancing debt, other senior claims of 40, and 50 million shares. Estimated NAV is (80 / 0.065 + 25 − 500 − 40) / 50 = approximately $14.315. At a hypothetical $12 share price, the modeled discount is 16.17%. Stress NOI to 68 and the cap rate to 7.5%; NAV becomes approximately $7.833. That same $12 price is then a 53.19% premium. A discount claim is therefore conditional on assumptions. Neither scenario supplies the cash needed for a principal repayment.

Specify falsifiers and the next evidence review

The optimistic maintenance thesis weakens if repeated annual spending remains above the reserve without identifiable expansion projects. The skeptical tenant thesis weakens if signed renewals commence, rent is collected, and incentives fit the modeled budget. Define observable evidence and a review date rather than moving the goalposts after results arrive. Use the SEC's filing guide to locate business risks, management discussion, financial statements, and notes, then check subsequent filings for developments. State which changes would alter the model. General economic commentary can frame uncertainty, but company-specific conclusions require company-specific evidence.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical case: Worked memo conclusion without a trade recommendation

In this hypothetical case, the 75% reported payout understates the 90% normalized post-refinancing payout. The downside case exceeds recurring owner earnings by 6, because 90 − 84 = 6. That is a recurring funding gap under the scenario, not proof of immediate insolvency. The base NAV discount disappears under the joint income and cap-rate stress. A rigorous conclusion is that dividend coverage depends materially on maintenance and tenant assumptions, while valuation depends on the selected income and required return. The next research priority is evidence that distinguishes those assumptions.

Test your reasoning

Holding all other downside assumptions fixed, how much additional recurring earnings would bring the downside payout ratio to 90% rather than 107.14%?

Reveal the worked answer

A 90 dividend at a 90% payout requires earnings of 90 / 0.90 = 100. The downside case has 84, so it needs 16 more recurring earnings. Merely closing the 6 funding gap would produce 90 of earnings and a 100% payout.

A common mistake

A detailed spreadsheet does not turn assumptions into facts; a valuation discount does not resolve a cash shortfall or establish a trading conclusion.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Separate sourced facts, management claims, assumptions, and calculations.
  • Write the strongest competing explanation.
  • Reconcile earnings, cash needs, and valuation independently.
  • Define observable evidence that would change each thesis.
  • State unresolved questions and a dated review trigger.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
