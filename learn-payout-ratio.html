Payout ratios: choose the denominator before judging safety

Compare FFO and AFFO coverage without confusing dividend policy with the REIT taxable-income distribution requirement.

A ratio with more than one denominator

A payout ratio divides dividends by an earnings or cash-generation measure. For property REITs, investors often examine FFO or AFFO payout ratios because GAAP earnings include real estate depreciation. These ratios answer different questions and can differ materially. Write the denominator in the label rather than saying only payout ratio. Use common dividends with income available to common shareholders, and match annual dividends to annual performance. A quarterly dividend annualized today is a forward assumption, not the same as dividends actually paid last year.

The tax rule is a separate calculation

The commonly cited U.S. REIT distribution requirement concerns at least 90% of qualifying taxable income, subject to the applicable tax rules. It does not mean a REIT must distribute 90% of FFO, AFFO, revenue, or market value. Taxable income and financial reporting measures can diverge. This educational distinction does not determine an individual investor's tax treatment. For a company's compliance discussion, read its tax section and distribution disclosures rather than reverse-engineering compliance from the AFFO payout ratio.

Coverage versus reinvestment flexibility

An income-focused reader may prefer a larger distribution today. A balance-sheet-focused reader may prefer retaining more funds to repair properties, repay debt, or avoid issuing shares. A lower ratio creates potential room but does not guarantee good capital allocation. A higher ratio can be manageable when cash flows are stable and investment needs are limited, yet leave little margin for a tenant default. Evaluate the ratio alongside the business model, recurring expenditure, debt calendar, and available liquidity rather than applying one universal safety cutoff.

A practical reading and stress workflow

Collect declared and paid common dividends, the issuer's FFO and AFFO reconciliations, and diluted share information. Separate special dividends from the ordinary run rate. Review whether AFFO excludes costs you consider recurring. Calculate coverage under both the reported measure and a conservative alternative. Then reduce income and increase interest costs in a simple sensitivity exercise. Check the board's declared distribution language: a historical payment pattern does not bind future decisions. Revisit the analysis when financing or lease conditions change.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical arithmetic: coverage under pressure

Assume annual ordinary dividends of $3 per share, FFO of $4.50, and AFFO of $4.00. FFO payout is 66.7%; AFFO payout is 75%. If AFFO declines 20% to $3.20 while the dividend stays $3, the AFFO ratio rises to 93.75%. At $2.80 AFFO it becomes 107.1%. This does not mechanically predict a cut, but it shows that the dividend would exceed this measure of recurring earnings. No taxable-income figure is supplied, so tax compliance cannot be inferred.

A common mistake

The 90% REIT taxable-income rule is not an FFO or AFFO payout target.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Name and verify the denominator.
  • Match periods and common-share claims.
  • Separate ordinary and special dividends.
  • Stress both earnings and financing costs.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
