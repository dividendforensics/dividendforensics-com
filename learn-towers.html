Tower REITs: colocations, carrier concentration and ground leases

Examine tower revenue growth through tenant additions, amendments, cancellations and the obligations beneath the infrastructure.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. The customer rents infrastructure access
  2. Disaggregate organic growth
  3. Competing views on infrastructure demand
  4. Land rights and cash-flow checks
  5. Worked example

The customer rents infrastructure access

Tower-focused REITs lease space and related rights to wireless operators that install equipment. Some telecommunications REIT portfolios also contain other infrastructure, so the segment mix matters. Multiple tenants can use one site, potentially improving its economics because certain costs are shared. A tower's value depends on usefulness within a communications network, available capacity, approvals and alternatives. Counting towers without considering their location, occupancy and ownership rights can obscure large differences in income quality.

Disaggregate organic growth

Revenue can increase through escalators, additional tenants and changes to existing equipment arrangements. It can decline through cancellations or decommissioning. Read whether organic growth excludes currency movements, acquisitions and pass-through revenue, and whether straight-line accounting affects the comparison. A large tenant may account for many individual leases, so thousands of contracts do not necessarily mean thousands of independent credit exposures. Carrier consolidation can reduce duplicate sites, and equipment upgrades do not automatically create the same economics at every location.

Competing views on infrastructure demand

The optimistic case emphasizes expanding data usage and the difficulty of replacing established sites. The cautious case emphasizes concentrated customers, network redesign, lease cancellations and differing economics across countries. Both perspectives require contract-level context. Higher data traffic is not identical to higher landlord rent: what the tenant may install and what triggers additional payments are contractual questions. International growth can diversify customers but introduces currency, regulatory and operating differences that deserve explicit treatment rather than being absorbed into a single growth rate.

Land rights and cash-flow checks

Determine whether the REIT owns the underlying land or leases it, and compare ground-lease duration with customer lease commitments. Review renewal options, escalation terms, remaining site rights and spending to extend control. Inspect customer concentration, churn assumptions, geographic exposure and debt currency. AFFO reconciliations should be read for recurring capital, straight-line rent and other adjustments; definitions are not uniform. Acquisition spending and development commitments are separate funding decisions, even when management presents attractive organic growth.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

Suppose a hypothetical site earns $30,000 annually from its first tenant and incurs $12,000 in direct site costs. Its simplified contribution is $18,000. A second tenant adds $20,000 of revenue and $3,000 of incremental cost, raising contribution to $35,000. Revenue grows about 66.7%, while contribution grows about 94.4%. This illustrates shared-cost economics, not a sector forecast; it excludes installation capital, overhead, financing, taxes and the possibility of later tenant cancellation.

A common mistake

Do not use mobile-data growth as a direct forecast for tower rent. Contract terms, customer concentration and the cost of controlling the site determine the connection.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Separate escalators, additions and cancellations.
  • Read customer concentration by revenue.
  • Compare ground and customer lease terms.
  • Assess currency exposure and AFFO adjustments.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
