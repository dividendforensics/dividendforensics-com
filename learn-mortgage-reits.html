Mortgage REITs: spreads, leverage and book-value risk

Understand why mortgage REITs require a financing and credit framework rather than the operating-property metrics used for equity REITs.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. Financing rather than collecting property rent
  2. Funding, hedging and liquidity interact
  3. Competing views on high distributions
  4. Use the issuer's actual framework
  5. Worked example
  6. Sources & next steps
01

Financing rather than collecting property rent

Mortgage REITs hold or originate mortgages and mortgage-related securities. Their business therefore differs from an equity REIT that owns buildings and leases space. Agency mortgage securities, non-agency securities and commercial mortgage loans have different risk profiles. Guarantees on qualifying agency securities can reduce specified credit risks but do not prevent market-price declines, financing stress or losses for common shareholders. Identify what assets the company actually holds before interpreting its dividend yield or comparing it with a property landlord.

02

Funding, hedging and liquidity interact

An mREIT may finance assets through collateralized borrowings such as repurchase agreements. Changes in collateral values or lender requirements can create demands for additional cash. Interest-rate hedges can offset selected exposures but are imperfect and may leave mortgage-spread, prepayment and other risks. When borrowers refinance more quickly or slowly than expected, asset cash flows change. Examine asset duration, funding terms, unpledged liquidity and sensitivity disclosure together; a narrow focus on the current interest spread misses the balance-sheet mechanism.

03

Competing views on high distributions

A favorable view emphasizes income earned from a carefully financed portfolio and potential improvement when market conditions stabilize. A skeptical view emphasizes leverage, credit losses, spread volatility and the possibility that distributions coexist with declining book value. A high dividend yield can reflect a low share price rather than a safer income stream. Compare dividends with changes in per-share book value over a consistent period, while recognizing that book value itself depends on accounting and valuation choices. Neither measure independently predicts future returns.

04

Use the issuer's actual framework

Do not import equity-REIT FFO or AFFO comparisons mechanically. Mortgage REITs may present distributable earnings, earnings available for distribution or other adjusted measures with different definitions. Read the reconciliation, credit-loss treatment and realized-versus-unrealized adjustments. For loan portfolios, review loan-to-value assumptions, borrower concentration, nonaccruals, modifications and funding commitments. For securities portfolios, review leverage, hedges, spread sensitivity and repo exposure. Issuer filings are essential because superficially similar companies may pursue materially different strategies and bear different risks.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

Assume a simplified hypothetical balance sheet holds $1 billion of mortgage assets funded by $800 million of debt and $200 million of equity. A 3% asset-value decline is $30 million. With debt unchanged and no offsetting hedge gains, equity falls to $170 million, a 15% decline. This is an illustration of leverage, not a forecast. Actual hedges, income, collateral requirements, valuation methods and asset sales can change both the amount and timing of the outcome.

A common mistake

Agency credit support does not protect a common shareholder from interest-rate, mortgage-spread or liquidity risk. A large distribution should not replace analysis of total economic performance.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Identify asset type and credit exposure.
  • Read leverage and collateral requirements.
  • Inspect hedges and spread sensitivities.
  • Reconcile adjusted earnings and book-value changes.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
