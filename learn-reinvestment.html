Dividend reinvestment: compounding with assumptions visible

Understand what a DRIP changes, what it cannot fix, and how to interpret a constant-yield reinvestment model.

  2. A smooth model is a controlled experiment
  3. Tax and concentration do not disappear

  5. Worked example
  6. Sources & next steps

Reinvestment changes the number of shares

A dividend reinvestment plan uses distributions to purchase additional shares under the plan's terms. If a payment is reinvested at a lower price, it can buy more shares; at a higher price, fewer. Fractional-share handling, fees and the execution date depend on the broker or issuer. The key distinction is that receiving cash and reinvesting cash are two steps. A dividend is not an extra return that can be added on top of a total-return figure already including distributions.

A smooth model is a controlled experiment

The calculator on this site applies a constant annual distribution yield to the modeled balance in monthly periods, with optional monthly contributions and a user-set withholding assumption. That makes the compounding arithmetic inspectable. Real prices, dividend rates, tax treatment and payment schedules vary. A constant-yield model does not predict a particular REIT's share count or future market price. Use it to understand the effect of time, contributions and reinvestment, then stress the inputs.

Tax and concentration do not disappear

Reinvestment may still involve taxable distributions depending on jurisdiction and account type. It can also increase exposure to the same issuer without a fresh purchase decision. An investor who already has a concentrated position may prefer to receive cash and allocate it elsewhere. Another may value automation and fractional purchases. Compare those approaches against transaction costs, diversification and cash needs rather than assuming reinvestment is always superior.

Read results as scenarios, not targets

A scenario with an unchanged payout is optimistic if cash generation is deteriorating. A dividend cut reduces the cash available for future purchases, and a permanent decline in asset value can overwhelm years of reinvestment. Separate your contributions from modeled investment income when reviewing a final balance. A larger balance funded mostly by additional savings is not evidence of extraordinary performance. Vary one assumption at a time so the source of a change remains understandable.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical: the first monthly step

Start with $10,000 at a constant 6% annual yield and assume no withholding. The first monthly distribution in this simplified model is $10,000 × 6% ÷ 12 = $50. Reinvesting it creates a $10,050 balance before any end-of-month contribution. This is a modeling convention, not an actual monthly payment schedule or price forecast.

A common mistake

A smooth rising chart reflects smooth assumptions. It does not demonstrate that the investment is safe or that income will remain constant.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Check the broker or issuer's reinvestment terms and fees.
  • State whether yield, dividend per share or share price is held constant.
  • Separate contributions from reinvested income.
  • Consider concentration, taxes and the possibility of a dividend cut.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
