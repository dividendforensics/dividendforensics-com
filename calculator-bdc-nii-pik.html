BDC NII, PIK & distribution worksheet

Separate regular and total common-distribution coverage, then trace a simple PIK-deduction sensitivity on a consistent period and unit basis.

By Jeong-Mo Goo · Published 18 October 2026 · Updated 18 October 2026

The question to answer.

Does the reported income cover the regular distribution and the entire distribution on the same basis? What changes if a stated portion of recognized PIK is deducted as a simple sensitivity?

Educational scenario, not a forecast or investment recommendation. All prefilled amounts are synthetic. Editing them creates an unverified user scenario. Calculations stay in this browser tab; no inputs are stored or transmitted.

Your assumptions

Inputs are total amounts. Do not paste per-share income into an amount field. Use one currency and scale throughout.

The analysis as-of date must be on or after the reporting period end. For balance-sheet stock measures, period fields identify the reporting context, not an averaging interval. Preserve actual snapshot and market-quote dates in each source note.

Keep every input on the period, basis and unit selected below. Source links are notes for your own audit, not a claim of verification.

Define the inputs before comparing.

Net investment income is an accounting income measure after applicable investment expenses; it is not a cash-flow statement. Payment-in-kind income can enter recognized income before cash is received. Use the amount included in the chosen NII, rather than the balance of PIK-bearing investments. Separate ordinary, supplemental and special common distributions instead of comparing earnings only with the smallest visible payment.

The calculation.

Total distributions = regular + supplemental + special. Regular coverage = NII / regular distributions. Total coverage = NII / total distributions. PIK/TII = PIK income / total investment income. PIK/NII is interpreted only if NII is positive. At deduction fraction f, sensitivity income = NII − PIK × f; coverage divides this hypothetical income by the matching distribution.

Formula version bdc-1.0.1. Decimal arithmetic uses 40 significant digits without intermediate display rounding. Results display four decimal places, half up; very small nonzero values use scientific notation. Blank or unavailable inputs stay missing; zero denominators produce “Not defined” with a reason.

Synthetic example — not company results

Reproduce the arithmetic.

Synthetic quarter, USD million: NII 120, included PIK 18, total investment income 180, regular distribution 100, supplemental 10 and special 5. Total distributions are 115. Regular coverage is 1.2000× and total coverage is 1.0435×. PIK/TII is 10.0000%, while PIK/NII is 15.0000%. At 25%/50%/100% PIK deductions, income is 115.5/111/102 and total coverage is 1.0043×/0.9652×/0.8870×. These are deliberately hypothetical inputs, not a company record.

Where to find the inputs.

Start with the statement of operations for NII and total investment income. Follow the investment-income accounting policy and PIK disclosure to establish what was recognized during the exact period. Find common distributions in the changes in net assets, distribution note and declarations. A cash-flow statement may show cash paid on a different timetable. Check declared versus paid basis and distinguish common from preferred distributions before copying values.

Record each source URL, accession if applicable, table or note location, original period and scale. Preserve the issuer definition. The source record below each result distinguishes manual entry from review: no reviewer or review date is invented.

Interpret the result with its denominator.

Regular coverage and total coverage are both useful because a supplemental distribution changes the denominator. Neither ratio establishes the tax character, sustainability or timing of cash payments. A negative NII produces signed negative coverage rather than a hidden zero. If supplemental or special distributions are unknown, leave them blank: total coverage remains undefined while regular coverage can still be calculated. An explicit zero is a separate user assumption.

What this worksheet leaves out.

Deducting PIK is a simple sensitivity, not cash earnings, operating cash flow or cash available for distributions. It holds expenses, incentive fees, taxes, collections and other noncash items constant. The 100% column is not a default or loss forecast. Total-amount mode avoids silently dividing PIK by current shares and comparing it with NII calculated using weighted-average shares. Reported and issuer-adjusted NII cannot be mixed. No recommendation, safety score or tax distribution test is generated.

No automatic currency, share-count, quarter/YTD or adjusted-to-reported conversion occurs. The common context is a user assertion, not evidence that pasted figures match. Check each original disclosure yourself; a manual input is not a verified company result.

Questions worth asking.

Why is an unknown supplement not zero?

Zero asserts there was no amount. A blank means the amount has not been established; the total cannot be completed without an assumption.

Can I use a paid distribution?

Yes, select paid and apply that basis to every distribution input. Paid cash can relate to an earlier declaration, so the time comparison needs explanation.
