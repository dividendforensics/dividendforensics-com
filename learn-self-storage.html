Self-storage REITs: street rates, existing rents and turnover

Learn why storage occupancy and advertised move-in prices can send different signals about revenue and operating performance.

DFB Education · Intermediate learning path · 3 min read · Updated 18 Oct 2026 · Editorial approach
In this guide
  1. Small spaces with short commitments
  2. Street rates are not portfolio rents
  3. Competing views on pricing power
  4. Read the operating and ownership details
  5. Worked example
  6. Sources & next steps
01

Small spaces with short commitments

Self-storage operators rent units to households and businesses. Demand can come from moving, household changes, renovation and business storage, but the importance of each driver varies locally. Short rental arrangements allow prices to change relatively quickly. That flexibility works in both directions: competitors can discount available units, and customers can leave. Unit size, climate control, accessibility and location affect achieved rent, so a simple facility count or total square footage does not fully describe the portfolio.

02

Street rates are not portfolio rents

The advertised price for a new customer may differ from what existing customers pay. Introductory discounts, promotions and later increases complicate the comparison. Track move-in rates, existing-customer revenue trends, occupancy and move-outs together. A landlord can maintain revenue temporarily through increases to existing customers even when marginal new-customer pricing weakens. That may be sustainable or may lead to higher departures. A useful analysis asks how long customers stay and what replacement tenants are paying, where that information is disclosed.

03

Competing views on pricing power

The constructive argument is that moving stored possessions is inconvenient and customers may value proximity more than a small price difference. The skeptical argument notes that households under pressure can empty units and competing facilities can offer aggressive promotions. New supply is intensely local: a facility across town may matter less than one nearby. Short leases can accelerate recovery when demand improves but do not insulate the business from weak housing turnover, customer affordability pressures or an oversupplied neighborhood.

04

Read the operating and ownership details

Inspect same-store occupancy, realized rent, promotional activity, expenses and development lease-up. Determine whether management fees come from properties owned by others, and distinguish those revenues from owned-property rental income. New stores may dilute aggregate occupancy while adding future potential, so compare stabilized and lease-up pools carefully. Review recurring maintenance and major improvements before accepting an AFFO figure. Property taxes, insurance and marketing costs can rise even when rent slows, making the expense bridge essential to understanding cash flow.

Work through the numbers

Hypothetical worked example

A hypothetical facility has 500 identical units renting for an average collected $120 monthly at 90% occupancy. Annual revenue is 500 × 90% × $120 × 12 = $648,000. If collected rent rises 5% to $126 but occupancy falls to 85%, revenue becomes $642,600, about 0.83% lower. This simplified example assumes stable rates throughout the year and excludes fees, promotions and expenses. It demonstrates why price increases cannot be evaluated independently of customer retention.

A common mistake

A higher existing-customer rate does not prove stronger new demand. Compare achieved prices and occupancy, and keep stabilized properties separate from new facilities still leasing up.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Compare move-in and existing-customer pricing.
  • Read occupancy and same-store definitions.
  • Inspect nearby supply and promotions.
  • Separate owned properties from management fees.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
