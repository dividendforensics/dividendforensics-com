Expert workshop: normalize AFFO across competing definitions

Rebuild two hypothetical issuer reconciliations, distinguish owner earnings from cash timing, and test whether apparent dividend coverage survives normalization.

01

Prerequisites and the analytical contract

Complete the FFO, AFFO, payout-ratio, and dilution guides first. This workshop assumes you can trace a reconciliation from net income and distinguish diluted shares from shares outstanding. The assignment is to compare two hypothetical reporting policies for the same underlying property economics. All amounts below are invented millions of dollars. The goal is a reproducible comparison, not a new universal AFFO definition. Nareit explicitly notes the absence of a standardized AFFO definition. Keep the issuer's reported measure intact and place your analytical alternative beside it, with each adjustment explained and reversible.

02

Build two reconciliations from identical economics

Assume both businesses have FFO of 120, including a stock-compensation expense of 4. Both recognize 6 of noncash straight-line rental revenue. Actual recurring building maintenance is 14 and recurring tenant improvements and commissions are 6. Policy A deducts an estimated maintenance reserve of 8, deducts the rent adjustment of 6, and adds back stock compensation of 4. Its reported AFFO is 120 − 8 − 6 + 4 = 110. Policy B deducts actual maintenance and leasing costs totaling 20 and the rent adjustment, while retaining stock compensation expense: 120 − 20 − 6 = 94.

03

Normalize without double counting

Choose an owner-earnings policy that retains the stock-compensation expense and deducts the actual recurring investment of 20. Start from A's 110, reverse its compensation add-back of 4, add back its already deducted reserve of 8, then deduct actual expenditure of 20: 110 − 4 + 8 − 20 = 94. B already equals 94. Simply subtracting 20 from A would count the reserve twice. For a separate current-cash proxy, add back the noncash compensation to the normalized 94, obtaining 98 before working capital and other cash items. Label 98 separately; it does not erase the ownership cost of employee shares.

04

Competing interpretations need evidence

Management might defend the reserve because replacements are lumpy and one heavy spending year overstates normal requirements. An owner might reject it because repeated catch-up projects reveal chronic under-reserving. Resolve that disagreement with a multi-year maintenance schedule, building age, lease obligations, and actual occupancy-preservation spending. Likewise, compensation is noncash in the current period but economically relevant to owners. Retaining the accounting expense is one imperfect proxy for that cost; a dilution-based scenario is another. Do not automatically subtract both the full compensation expense and a cash amount for repurchasing every related share without explaining the overlap.

05

A source-reading audit trail

Read each real issuer's net-income-to-FFO bridge before its AFFO bridge. Record the exact fiscal period, ownership basis, adjustments, and whether recurring capital expenditure is estimated or actual. One Liberty's filed annual report provides a primary example of an AFFO policy that adds back restricted-stock compensation and warns that AFFO does not cover every cash need. It is an illustration of why definitions matter, not the source of our fictional policies or numbers. Link expenditure classifications to cash-flow and property notes; preserve unresolved items rather than silently treating missing disclosure as zero.

06

Stress coverage and state the limits

With annual common dividends of 90, A's reported payout is 90 / 110 = 81.8%, while normalized owner-earnings payout is 90 / 94 = 95.7%. The current-cash proxy gives 90 / 98 = 91.8%, still before other cash movements. If recurring investment rises another 5, normalized earnings become 89 and payout reaches 101.1%. This does not mechanically prove a dividend cut. Cash reserves, financing, timing, and taxable income remain separate questions. Actual spending also includes judgment: distinguish maintenance from genuine expansion, and test the boundary rather than claiming an exact, objective answer.

Work through the numbers

Worked hypothetical bridge: the apparent advantage disappears

Policy A appears to have 20 of coverage above a 90 dividend; Policy B has only 4. After applying the same expense policy, both have 94 of owner earnings and a 4 cushion. The entire 16 difference is explained by the 4 stock-compensation add-back and the 12 shortfall between the 8 reserve and 20 actual recurring spending. There is no operating advantage in this deliberately identical case. With 25 million diluted shares each, reported A AFFO is $4.40 per share, but normalized earnings for both are $3.76.

Test your reasoning

If actual recurring expenditure were 16 instead of 20, with all other assumptions unchanged, what would normalized earnings and the payout ratio be?

Reveal the worked answer

Normalized earnings would be 120 − 6 − 16 = 98. Starting with Policy A gives the same answer: 110 − 4 + 8 − 16 = 98. Payout would be 90 / 98 = 91.84%.

A common mistake

Subtracting actual maintenance without reversing a reserve already deducted double counts expenditure; calling the compensation add-back free cash ignores dilution.

What to check in the disclosures

  • Reconcile both policies to the same FFO and ownership basis.
  • Reverse existing reserves before replacing them.
  • Separate economic compensation cost from current cash timing.
  • Document recurring versus growth expenditure judgments.
  • Publish reported, normalized, and stressed coverage separately.

Sources & scope

This guide focuses on US REITs. Examples are hypothetical unless explicitly attributed. Definitions and issuer disclosures can change; use the current source and the issuer's own reconciliation.
